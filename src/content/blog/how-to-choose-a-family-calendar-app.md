It's Sunday at 8:45 p.m. The kids are finally asleep, and you're on the couch with your phone, scrolling back through a week of email to find the spirit day, the field trip form and the new practice time. You find most of them. You'll find the last one on Thursday, at the exact moment it's too late.

If you're wondering how to choose a family calendar app, here's the short answer: don't pick the one with the longest feature list. Pick the one that fixes the part of your week that hurts most, and test it small, for one week, before you move your whole family over. That keeps the risk tiny and the decision easy.

I'm a dad of two. I stuck with a system that didn't work for way too long, mostly because switching felt like more work than suffering. Here's what finally got me to try something new, and the test I'd use on any app, including mine.

## Your current system isn't free

Most families run on some mix of a paper calendar, a group text, one parent's memory and a Gmail search bar. It feels free because nobody sends you a bill.

But it does cost something. It costs Sunday nights spent hunting through email. It costs the missed date that turns into a scramble, a late pickup or a kid in jeans on pajama day. And it costs the quiet mental load of being the one person who knows everything, because if you stop knowing, nobody does.

Before you choose anything new, write down what your system costs you in a normal week. Be specific: how many minutes, how many near misses, how many "wait, when is that?" texts. That list is what a new app has to beat.

## How to choose a family calendar app: start with where your dates come from

Every family calendar app is good at something. The question is whether it's good at your problem.

1. **If your dates are mostly typed in by hand** (doctor visits, birthdays, weekend plans), a simple shared calendar may be all you need.
2. **If your dates come from other calendars** (a team app, a school calendar feed, work), look for one that can pull those in, and check whether changes flow both ways.
3. **If your dates arrive in emails** (the school, the coach, the dance studio, camp), look for an app that finds the dates in your email, because typing them in is the part nobody keeps up with.
4. **If you need lists, meals or chat in the same place**, give that weight, but don't let it bury the main job.

Then check the boring stuff: does it work on your phones, does it sync with the calendar you already use, how many people does one plan cover, and what does it cost after the trial.

## The one-week test: one kid, one activity

Here's the test I'd run on any new app. It takes about 20 minutes to set up and one normal week to judge.

1. **Pick one kid and one activity.** Just soccer, or just the class, not everything. Small enough to set up in one sitting.
2. **Run the new app next to your old system.** Don't delete anything. You're comparing, not committing.
3. **Keep a tiny scorecard.** Each time a date comes up, note whether the new app caught it, whether you had to add it yourself, and how long that took.
4. **Show it to one other person.** Another household member, a grandparent who does pickups, whoever asks you "what time is practice?" the most.
5. **Decide at the end of the week.** If it caught more, saved time and the other person actually looked at it, expand to the next kid or activity. If not, cancel before the trial ends and you've lost nothing but a few minutes.

The point is to make "trying something" cheap. A two-week trial with a one-week test is a lot less scary than "reorganize the whole family."

## The real worries about switching (and how to handle them)

### "Setup will take forever"

It shouldn't, if you start with one kid and one activity. If an app needs an afternoon of data entry before it does anything useful, that tells you something. A good app should show you its value in the first session.

### "My family won't use it"

They don't have to love it. They just have to find one answer faster than texting you. Show them the one screen that answers the question they ask most, usually "what's happening this week?" If the app puts dates on the Google Calendar they already check, they may not need to open anything new at all.

### "I don't want an app in my email"

Fair. Before you connect anything, read exactly what it can see and what it can change. Look for read-only access, a clear list of what it reads, and a way to disconnect. If an app can't explain that in plain words, skip it.

### "What if I hate it?"

Then you cancel during the trial and go back to what you had, which you never turned off. That's the whole reason to run the test next to your old system.
