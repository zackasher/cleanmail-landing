A 2-2-5-5 parenting schedule splits custody in a repeating two-week cycle: two days with one parent, two with the other, then five and five. Each parent keeps the same two weekdays every week, and the weekends alternate. This guide shows how the rotation works, how it compares to 2-2-3 and 3-4-4-3, and how to choose.

## Key takeaways

- A 2-2-5-5 schedule repeats every 14 days. Each parent keeps the same two weekdays every week, and the weekends alternate.
- 2-2-5-5 and 5-2-2-5 are the same rotation counted from a different day.
- The longest stretch away from either parent is five days, with four handoffs every two weeks.
- A 2-2-3 rotation keeps every stretch to three days or less, at the cost of more handoffs.
- Both parents have school days every week, so school and activity emails and event dates must reach both households.

## What is a 2-2-5-5 parenting schedule?

A 2-2-5-5 schedule is a 50/50 custody arrangement where kids spend two days with one parent, two with the other, then five and five, repeating every fourteen days. It is one of the common equal-time rotations, alongside 2-2-3 and 3-4-4-3.

### The repeating two-week pattern

Parent A always has Monday and Tuesday. Parent B always has Wednesday and Thursday. The weekends, Friday through Sunday, alternate. So in week one, Parent A has Friday to Sunday and keeps the kids through Tuesday: a five-day stretch. In week two, Parent B has Friday to Sunday after their Wednesday and Thursday: their five-day stretch.

### Who has which days: a worked example

Say the cycle starts on Monday, March 2. Parent A has March 2 and 3, Parent B has March 4 and 5, and Parent A has March 6 through 10 (the weekend plus Monday and Tuesday). Parent B then has March 11 through 15 (Wednesday, Thursday and their weekend), and the cycle starts again on Monday, March 16. Because the weekdays never change hands, standing activities like Tuesday piano always land with the same parent.

## How does 2-2-5-5 compare to 3-2-2-3 and 5-2-2-5?

5-2-2-5 is not a different schedule. It is the same rotation counted from a different day, so everything here about 2-2-5-5 applies to it.

3-2-2-3 is another name for the 2-2-3 rotation: two days, two days, then three, with the three-day block alternating each week. It is the real alternative to 2-2-5-5.

### Side-by-side pattern comparison

| Schedule | Cycle | Handoffs per 2 weeks | Longest stretch | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2-2-5-5 (also 5-2-2-5) | 14 days | 4 | 5 days | Families who want fixed weekdays and alternating weekends |
| 2-2-3 (also 3-2-2-3) | 14 days | 6 | 3 days | Younger kids who do better with short gaps |
| 3-4-4-3 | 14 days | 4 | 4 days | Families who want one midweek handoff a week |

### What each schedule looks like on an actual calendar

Put 2-2-5-5 in a shared calendar and you get a steady shape: the same two weekdays in each color every week, plus one long weekend block that alternates. Field trips, picture day and permission slips land on whichever parent holds that day, so both homes need the dates.

A 2-2-3 spreads the days more evenly but moves the kids more often, so there are more handoffs to coordinate, not fewer. Pick by counting the handoffs you can actually run and the stretch length your kids handle well.

## Which schedule fits your family situation?

The right schedule depends on two things: how your kids handle time away from each parent, and how much of your week is run by activities.

### Activity-heavy kids: why 2-2-5-5 often wins

If your kids have practice, lessons or tutoring on fixed weekdays, 2-2-5-5 keeps each parent on the same weekdays every week. The carpool parent and the pickup time stay with one household, week after week.

### Kids who struggle with long stretches away: why 2-2-3 wins

Young children often do better with shorter gaps between homes. A 2-2-3 keeps every stretch to three days or less, while 2-2-5-5 has a five-day stretch with each parent every two weeks. If your kids are small and fall apart after several days away, the extra handoffs of a 2-2-3 may be worth it.

### One midweek handoff: 3-4-4-3

If you want fewer midweek exchanges, 3-4-4-3 alternates three- and four-day blocks with one handoff in the middle of each week. The trade is that the days each parent has shift from week to week.

## What does a full month of 2-2-5-5 look like?

The pattern repeats every 14 days, so once you see one cycle on a calendar, the whole month is predictable. Parent A has every Monday and Tuesday, Parent B has every Wednesday and Thursday, and the weekends alternate. Exchange days stay fixed, so pickups and drop-offs never move.

### A sample two-week cycle on the calendar

Block out the two fixed weekday pairs and the alternating weekends as repeating events first. Each parent ends up with seven of every fourteen nights, which is what makes the split 50/50. The only thing that changes from month to month is which parent has a given weekend.

### Where school events and practices land

Picture day, field trips and permission slips don't care whose day it is. Put each school event on the shared household calendar with the kid's name attached, so the parent on duty that day knows they're driving. Practices recur on the same weekdays, so on a 2-2-5-5 they stay with the same parent.

## What are the tradeoffs of a 2-2-5-5 schedule?

### The upside: fixed weekdays and a long weekend each

Each parent always has the same two weekdays, which makes work travel, activities and standing plans easy to set up. Both parents also get a long weekend block every two weeks.

### The downside: a five-day stretch and midweek handoffs

Each parent goes five days without the kids every two weeks, which can be hard on young children. There are four handoffs every two weeks, and the midweek ones mean backpacks, permission slips and homework moving between houses. Both parents have school days every week, so both need the school and activity emails, not just the schedule-keeper.

Most parenting plans add rules for holidays, birthdays and trips that take priority over the rotation. If yours does not, add them before you start.

## How do you run a 50/50 schedule when school emails only reach one parent?

### The copy-by-hand problem before the week starts

On a 2-2-5-5, school and activity email lands in one inbox while the kids are at the other house part of every week. The schedule-keeper copies dates into a calendar by hand, and the co-parent finds out about picture day when it is already over. Deadlines arrive scattered across separate emails, which is why hand-copying breaks down.

## How do you set up and adjust a 2-2-5-5 schedule?

### Anchoring the start date and exchange times

Pick a fixed start date and write down which weekdays belong to each parent and which weekend starts the cycle. Most families anchor exchanges to school pickup, so handoffs happen at the school rather than in the middle of the workday. Put the full rotation on a shared calendar both parents can see, so neither of you is texting "whose day is it?" from the pickup line.

### Handling holidays, breaks and trade requests

Decide before you start whether holidays override the rotation or alternate by year. Spring break and winter break usually get their own rules. For one-off swaps, keep it simple: a written request, a written yes, and the calendar updated by whoever asked. If school and activity emails only reach one of you, forward the ones with dates so the shared calendar stays true for both households.
