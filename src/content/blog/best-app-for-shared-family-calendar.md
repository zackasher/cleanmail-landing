The best app for a shared family calendar depends on your devices. All-iPhone households should use Apple Calendar with iCloud and Family Sharing. All-Android households should use a shared Google Calendar. Mixed households should run Google Calendar as the hub. This guide walks through setup for each, then gets to the real bottleneck: the dates that live in school and sports email and never make it onto any calendar on their own.

## Key takeaways

- All-iPhone households can use the built-in iCloud calendar. Turning on Family Sharing creates a shared "Family" calendar automatically.
- Mixed Android and iPhone households should run Google Calendar as the single shared hub instead of mirroring events across two calendar systems.
- Google Calendar is free, works on both platforms, and lets you color-code events by kid.
- A shared calendar solves seeing the schedule. It does not solve getting the dates out of school and activity email, which is where most of the work is.
- When switching apps, export to an .ics file, import into the new app, and keep the old calendar read-only for a month as a safety net.

## What is the best app for a shared family calendar?

It comes down to two things: which phones are in your house, and where your time actually goes. If everyone uses iPhone, the iCloud calendar is free and already installed. If anyone uses Android, Google Calendar is the one app that works the same for everybody.

Then be honest about the bottleneck. If the problem is getting your partner and kids to look at the calendar, any of these works. If the problem is that one person reads every school email and types the dates in, no calendar app fixes that by itself.

## Which app is best when everyone uses iPhone?

Use the built-in iCloud calendar. When you set up Family Sharing, Apple creates a shared calendar called "Family" and adds everyone in your family group to it. You can also create your own iCloud calendar and share it with specific people.

Family Sharing adds more on top, like shared purchases, a shared photo album and location sharing, but the calendar part is still the iCloud calendar underneath. Turn on Family Sharing for the extras and use the Family calendar for events.

The limit: iCloud is good at showing the schedule and does nothing about the email. Someone still reads the newsletter, finds the picture day date and types it in.

### When a dedicated family organizer makes sense

Apps like Cozi earn their place when you want more than dates: shared grocery and to-do lists, a recipe box and a family-friendly view that a less techy partner will actually open. Cozi has a free version with ads, Cozi Gold at $39 a year, and Cozi Max at $79 a year, which adds an AI Event Import that turns emailed invitations into calendar events (prices from Cozi's plan page, November 2026).

## Which calendar works best for households with Android and iPhone?

Google Calendar. It runs natively on Android, has a solid iPhone app, and sits right next to Gmail, where most school email already lands. If both parents are on Android, it is the easiest shared calendar to set up.

### The mixed-household problem: two calendar systems, one schedule

The friction starts when one parent is on iPhone and the other is not. iCloud calendars share smoothly between Apple devices, but sharing one with an Android user means a public, view-only link. The Android parent can see the schedule but cannot add to it.

So the clean setup for mixed households is one Google Calendar that everyone shares. The iPhone parent can add their Google account to the built-in Calendar app or use the Google Calendar app. Don't try to mirror events across two systems. Two systems is how "wait, which calendar was that on?" starts.

## How do you create a shared family calendar in Google Calendar?

1. Open Google Calendar on a computer. Next to "Other calendars," click the plus sign and choose "Create new calendar." Name it something plain, like "Family."
2. Hover over the new calendar in the left sidebar, click the three dots and choose "Settings and sharing."
3. Under "Share with specific people or groups," add your partner's email address and choose "Make changes and manage sharing" so either of you can fix a date or add someone later.
4. Set a default notification on that calendar, such as a pop-up the evening before. It lands while there is still time to find the gym shoes.
5. Pick a color per kid when you add events, so one look at the month shows who has what.

### Settings worth checking on day one

Turn off email notifications for event changes on the shared calendar, or your inbox fills with noise. Leave "Make available to public" unchecked so only the people you added can see it.

Google Calendar can add some events from Gmail on its own, like flights and restaurant reservations. It will not pull the field trip date out of the third paragraph of a school newsletter. The setup above makes the calendar shared. It does nothing about getting events into it.

## How do you set up a shared calendar on iPhone?

Option 1: Family Sharing. Go to Settings, tap your name, tap Family Sharing and invite your family. A shared "Family" calendar appears in the Calendar app for everyone in the group.

Option 2: share one calendar. In the Calendar app, tap Calendars, tap Add Calendar and create a new iCloud calendar. Tap the info button next to it, tap Add Person and enter your partner's Apple Account email. They accept the invite and the calendar appears alongside their own.

One gotcha: if someone has notifications turned off for shared calendars, new events arrive silently. Check Settings, Notifications, Calendar on each phone. Changes can also take a few minutes to show up on the other device, so a missing event may just be delayed.

## What should you look for in a shared family calendar?

Judge any app on four things:

- **Who can edit.** Your partner should be able to add and change events, not just look.
- **Reminders per person.** An alert on your phone does nothing for the parent who is actually driving. Each person should get their own.
- **Sorting by kid.** With two or more kids across schools and teams, a flat list stops being readable. Color or labels per child matter.
- **How events get in.** This is the one most guides skip. Every calendar shows dates well. Very few help you get them there.

## Do you need a paid app, or is Google Calendar enough?

Free is genuinely enough if someone is happy to keep it updated. Google Calendar costs nothing, works on Android and iPhone, and a shared calendar color-coded by kid covers seeing the schedule and getting reminders.

Paid organizers add structure on top: lists, meal planning, a simpler family view and, in some cases, help turning emails into events. They are worth it when the upkeep is the part that hurts.

## Why doesn't a shared calendar fix the real problem?

A shared calendar only works after the events are in it. Your family's schedule doesn't start in a calendar app. It starts in the school newsletter, the coach's email, the PTA reminder and the dance studio update. Somebody reads all of it, finds the dates and types them in. Usually it is the same parent every time, and that parent becomes the person everyone asks.

That is the gap Cleanmail was built for. You pick the school, sports and activity senders in Gmail. Cleanmail reads only those, turns them into one short digest sorted by kid, and puts the dates on a calendar everyone at home shares. It also syncs to Google Calendar, so nobody has to learn a new place to look. Up to 4 people in a household join free, and it works on iPhone and Android with Gmail.

### When you don't need a new tool

If you get one school email a week and your kids do one activity, keep typing the dates in. The case for automation is volume: several schools, several teams, more kids, more senders.

## How do you move a family calendar to a new app without losing events?

Export first, then import, then keep the old calendar around.

In Google Calendar, go to Settings, then Import & export, then Export. You get a .ics file for each calendar. In iCloud on the web, you can share a calendar as a public link and subscribe to it, or open the calendar on a Mac and use File, then Export. In the new app, import the .ics file. Spot-check a few repeating events afterward, since repeats are what most often break.

Then leave the old calendar visible and read-only for a month. Don't delete it yet. If the band concert seems to be missing, you'll find it in the old calendar instead of finding out the hard way.
